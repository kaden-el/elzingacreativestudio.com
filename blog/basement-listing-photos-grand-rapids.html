How to Photograph a Basement for Your Grand Rapids Listing

Kaden Elzinga November 18, 2026  ·  5 min read

A buyer scrolls through your Grand Rapids listing. Eighteen bright photos of the kitchen, the living room and the backyard. Then one dark, crooked shot of a basement with a water heater in the corner. That one photo tells them more than you meant to say.

In West Michigan, almost every home has a basement, and buyers care about it. It is often the difference between a three-bedroom house and one that feels like it has room to grow. A basement photo needs to earn its place in the set.

Why basement photos go wrong

Basements have the least light in the house. Small windows sit high on the wall, and the ceiling is lower than upstairs. A phone camera reacts by turning everything gray or yellow.

Then there is the stuff. Basements collect boxes, bikes, holiday bins and old furniture. In person you walk past it. In a photo it is the first thing the eye lands on.

Finished basements: show the use

If the basement is finished, I shoot it like a real room with a job. A rec room needs to look like somewhere to watch a game. A guest bedroom needs a made bed. An office nook needs a clear desk.

I wait for the right angle. I stand in a corner, keep the camera level, and show the full length of the room so buyers understand the square footage. I turn on every lamp and overhead light, then blend the exposures so the windows are not blown out and the corners are not black.

Unfinished basements: shoot them honestly

Not every basement is a rec room, and that is fine. An unfinished basement with clean concrete walls, good headroom and a clear layout is a selling point for a buyer who wants to finish it themselves.

I shoot one or two wide frames, never ten. The goal is to show the size and the condition. I do not try to make it look like something it is not, because buyers will walk down those stairs at the showing anyway.

Prep that makes the biggest difference

Most of the work happens before I arrive. A short list goes a long way:

Clear the floor. Move boxes into one corner out of frame, or into the garage for the day. Put away anything on the laundry area that does not need to be there.

Check every bulb. Replace burned-out ones, and make sure they all match in color. Open the blinds on any window, even a small one.

Turn off the dehumidifier and any fans. The noise does not show in photos, but the cords and the machine do.

Be straight about moisture and radon

Many West Michigan basements deal with some moisture, and buyers know it. Do not hide it with photos. If there is a past issue that was fixed, tell your agent so it can be handled in the disclosure paperwork, not in the pictures.

Buyers also ask about radon in basements. The EPA has a plain-English guide at epa.gov/radon (https://www.epa.gov/radon) on testing and what the results mean. Photos do not replace a test, and I never stage around either topic.

Where basement photos go in the set

I put basement photos near the end of the gallery, after the main floor and bedrooms. Buyers should fall for the house first, then discover the bonus space. A finished basement gets three to five photos. An unfinished one gets one or two.

If the basement has a bathroom or a bar, those get their own frame. A floor plan also helps here, because it shows how the lower level connects to the rest of the house. I offer one for $75 as an add-on.

What I do on shoot day

I add the basement to the shot list when I book, so I know how much time to leave. It adds a few minutes, not an hour. Photos are delivered the next business day, and I travel free within roughly 25 miles of downtown Grand Rapids.

If you are not sure whether your basement is worth photographing, send me a quick phone picture beforehand. I will tell you straight what I would do with it.
