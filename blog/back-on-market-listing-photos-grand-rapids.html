Should You Get New Photos When a Listing Goes Back on Market in Grand Rapids?

Kaden Elzinga September 23, 2026  ·  5 min read

A house on the northeast side of Grand Rapids went under contract in eleven days this July. Multiple offers, no contingencies waived that mattered, everything pointed to a clean close. Then the buyer's lender pulled the loan three weeks later over a job change, and the listing bounced back onto the MLS in September with the exact same photos from midsummer. Green lawn, hanging baskets on the porch, a sky that doesn't match the calendar anymore.

Agents ask me some version of this every fall: does a back-on-market listing need new photos, or do the old ones still do the job? The honest answer depends on what changed between the first listing and the relist, and it's rarely the photos people assume matter most.

Buyers Read "Back on Market" Differently Than They Read "New Listing"

A relisted home doesn't get a clean slate. Buyers and their agents can see the listing history on Zillow and Realtor.com, and a status change from Pending back to Active raises a question before anyone reads the description: what happened?

Academic research on relisting backs this up. A study published in the Journal of Real Estate Finance and Economics found that homes pulled and relisted tend to sell for less than comparable homes that sold on the first try, even after controlling for price and condition. The listing photos are one of the few levers a seller has to counter that instinct, because they're the first thing a buyer actually evaluates.

The Photos Aren't Usually the Reason a Deal Fell Through

It's worth saying plainly: most fall-throughs are financing, inspection, or a buyer's personal situation changing, not a problem with how the home was presented. RISMedia's coverage of relisting data makes the same point — the reasons a home comes back on market are rarely about the listing itself.

That matters because it means a reshoot isn't a confession that something was wrong. It's a chance to make the relisting look like a new opportunity instead of a leftover.

When a Full Reshoot Is Worth It

A few situations make new photos an easy call. If the home was listed in late spring or summer and it's relisting in September or October, the exterior has visibly changed — different light, different landscaping, possibly leaves down or a bare tree line where there used to be green. West Michigan's shoulder season shows up fast in exterior shots.

It's also worth a reshoot if the home is now vacant when it was staged before, or staged when it was vacant before. Buyers scrolling photos notice when a listing's story doesn't match what they see on the walkthrough, and an outdated photo set creates that mismatch immediately.

And if the original photos were mediocre to begin with — flat lighting, a phone camera, an agent trying to save the cost — a relist is the natural moment to fix it. You're already re-marketing the home; there's no reason to re-market it with the same weak first impression.

When You Can Skip It

If the fall-through happened fast, the season hasn't shifted, staging hasn't changed, and the original photos were shot well, you probably don't need a full new set. A single fresh exterior shot with the current lawn and sky, swapped into the same gallery, can be enough to signal "this listing is current" without the cost of a full session.

I'd rather tell an agent to skip a reshoot they don't need than sell one for the sake of it. The goal is matching the photos to reality, not padding an invoice.

What a Refresh Shoot Actually Involves

A relisting refresh doesn't have to be the full package again. Most agents in this situation book an Essential shoot to cover the exterior and any rooms that changed, rather than redoing the whole interior from scratch. If the home has been sitting vacant since the fall-through, that's also a good time to add real twilight — it reads as a new listing in a way daytime photos alone don't.

Timing Matters More Than Usual Here

A relisted home has a narrower window than a fresh one. The listing history is already visible, so every extra day the gallery sits stale is a day buyers can point to and wonder why. Delivery on every shoot here is next business day, which matters more on a relist than a first listing — the faster the new photos are live, the faster the "back on market" flag stops being the headline.

Grand Rapids' shoulder season moves fast. A home that goes back on market in September and relists with July's photos is telling buyers, without meaning to, that nobody's paying close attention to this listing anymore. A short refresh shoot fixes that in one afternoon, and it's usually the cheapest thing you can do to change how a relisting gets read.

I've shot more relistings in the past few Septembers than I expected to, mostly because financing fell through on a summer contract and the seller wants back on the market before the market slows down for winter. It's a quick conversation every time: what actually changed since the first shoot, and does the gallery still tell the truth about the house. That's usually all it takes to decide.
