Real Estate Photography for Historic Homes in Grand Rapids

Kaden Elzinga August 26, 2026  ·  6 min read

I shot a listing on a tree-lined street in Heritage Hill last month, an 1895 Queen Anne with the original leaded glass still in the front windows. The agent's note said it would go fast. Then I walked in and found four-inch baseboards, a foyer feeding into three different doorways, and windows sized for 1895 heating bills instead of 2026 phone screens. Grand Rapids has more of these homes than almost any city its size, and real estate photography for a historic home does not work the same way it does for a five-year-old subdivision build. Shoot it like new construction and the photos undersell the house every time.

Why Century Homes Shoot Differently Than New Construction

New construction gives a photographer open floor plans, tall windows, and even light. A wide lens does most of the work, and the rooms forgive it.

A century home is the opposite. Rooms are compartmentalized because that is how houses were built before central air made open concept practical. Ceiling heights change floor to floor. Walls that look like they should come down are load-bearing. Push a wide-angle lens too hard in a room that size and you get bowed walls and a foyer that looks like a funhouse, not the historic details that are the whole reason someone is buying this house.

The Light Problem in Pre-War Interiors

Double-hung windows from 1900 are smaller than what gets installed today, and they're often set deep into thick plaster walls, which cuts the light again before it reaches the room. Dark original wood trim and built-ins absorb what light does get in instead of bouncing it back.

Straight HDR brackets can leave these rooms looking flat or leave the windows blown out to white. I shoot flambient on these jobs specifically because of this: a soft continuous LED fills the room evenly while I still keep the natural shadow and the actual view out the window. The trim reads dark and rich instead of muddy, which is the whole point of trim like that.

Curb Appeal Carries More Weight Here

A buyer searching for a home in Heritage Hill or Eastown is buying the era as much as the square footage. A wraparound porch, a turret, original clapboard or brick, and a mature tree canopy are the pitch before anyone opens the MLS photos of the kitchen.

That canopy is also the timing challenge. Big shade trees throw dappled, moving shadows across a facade at midday that a modern open lot never deals with. This is where a real twilight exterior earns its cost on a historic listing more than almost anywhere else — once the sky goes deep blue, porch lights and window glow do the work that direct sun can't do on a shaded lot.

A Floor Plan Earns Its Keep in an Odd Layout

Century homes get additions bolted on over decades. Staircases don't always land where a modern buyer expects, and the kitchen-to-dining flow can take a turn nobody could guess from the listing photos alone.

A 2D floor plan add-on is $75, and on a home like this it answers the "wait, how does this connect?" question before a buyer wastes a showing walking in circles. I add it on almost every historic listing I shoot now, even when the agent hadn't planned on it.

What Grand Rapids' Historic Districts Mean for a Listing

Heritage Hill alone holds roughly 1,300 buildings and is one of the largest urban historic districts in the country, protected as a National, State, and Local Historic District, according to the Heritage Hill Association. Grand Rapids also wrote Michigan's first historic preservation ordinance back in 1971 and now oversees six local historic districts citywide, per the Advisory Council on Historic Preservation.

A buyer who searches an address in one of these districts is going to find that history fast. If the listing photos don't match the character the house is known for, real estate photography that looks generic actively works against the sale instead of helping it.

Staging That Fits the House

I'm not the stager, but I flag it kindly when I show up to a vacant century home staged with a big modern gray sectional that fights a 1910 parlor's proportions. Doorways are narrower, room footprints are smaller, and furniture scaled for an open-concept build reads oversized and odd in these photos.

The listings that photograph best usually lean into the house instead of against it. A smaller sofa, a rug that doesn't fight the original floor, furniture placed to open up the room's actual shape rather than a generic "living room" arrangement.

What I Bring to a Century Home Shoot

No popping flash, no fisheye-wide lens choices, careful vertical correction so already-busy trim doesn't read as leaning walls. I also shoot extra frames of the details that got this house on someone's list in the first place: stained glass, pocket doors, built-in hutches, original hardware. Buyers of historic homes weight those details heavily, and an agent needs photos of them ready to go, not an afterthought shot on a phone during the showing.

Every century home in Grand Rapids photographs a little differently, and that's honestly my favorite part of this work. If your next listing is one of the city's older homes, tell me what's original before I show up so I can plan the shoot around it instead of working it out room by room on the fly.
