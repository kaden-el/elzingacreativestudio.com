Listing Photos vs Listing Video: Do West Michigan Sellers Need Both?

Kaden Elzinga June 16, 2026  ·  7 min read

Agents in West Michigan get pitched on video constantly. Every vendor, every industry webinar, every coaching call says video is non-negotiable now. I shoot both listing photos and listing video, so I have no financial reason to tell you one is better than the other. But after hundreds of listing shoots across Grand Rapids, the lakeshore, and the surrounding suburbs, I can tell you that the honest answer is more nuanced than most photographers will give you: photos are foundational for nearly every listing, and video earns its cost on specific ones. Knowing which category your listing falls into is worth more than buying both on autopilot.

Why listing photos are the non-negotiable baseline

Every MLS system in West Michigan requires photos. Buyers form their first impression in the photo gallery before they watch a video, click a tour, or read a description. The scroll stop happens at the first frame. A strong photo set is what gets a buyer to slow down long enough to care about everything else on the listing.

That first frame is also the image that appears on Zillow, Realtor.com, and any social share. It is the thumbnail on every agent's drip email and every Google search result. Video has no equivalent distribution surface at the top of the funnel — photos do. This is not an argument against video. It is an argument for understanding what photos actually do in the marketing funnel before deciding what else to add.

Where listing video actually moves the needle

Video earns its cost when the listing's story requires motion to tell. There are four categories where I consistently see video convert:

  • Luxury and high-end homes. Above roughly $600K in the Grand Rapids market, video signals seriousness. Buyers in this price range are comparing your listing against Ann Arbor, Chicago suburbs, and Holland waterfront. A professional 60-second walkthrough says this listing belongs in that tier.
  • Lakeshore and waterfront properties. Lake Michigan frontage, Gun Lake, Reeds Lake — these listings sell the water. A drone aerial and a smooth video pan of the water view communicate something a still photo cannot. The motion is the point.
  • Architecturally distinct homes. A home with a great floor plan, a dramatic entryway, or a connected indoor-outdoor flow reads better in video. Buyers understand the layout in a way they cannot from a sequence of stills.
  • Out-of-state and relocation buyers. A buyer relocating from Denver or Austin cannot easily drive over for a showing. A well-made listing video is the next best thing to walking the home, and it can move a remote buyer from "interested" to "booking a flight" faster than 40 photos can.

Where video is a nice-to-have, not a need

A median ranch in Kentwood, a townhome in Wyoming, a starter home in Allendale — these listings sell because they are priced right and the photos look clean. The buyer pool for these homes is mostly local. They will drive over. What moves the needle here is not video; it is having photos that look good on a phone at 9pm, a floor plan that answers the "is there a bedroom on the main level" question, and a fast turnaround that hits the list date.

Adding a $150 video to a $245,000 listing does not change the outcome when the bottleneck is offer volume driven by price and days on market, not engagement depth. Video is an awareness and consideration tool. On listings that already have enough buyer attention, you are spending money to engage buyers who were already going to show up.

The agent reel is a different product doing a different job

There is a meaningful distinction between a listing video (markets the house) and an agent reel (markets the agent). An on-camera reel that features you talking to camera, walking through a neighborhood, or explaining the offer process builds your personal brand over time. It does not sell this specific listing. It sells you to the next five buyers and sellers who find you on Instagram.

The West Michigan market reality right now

The Grand Rapids metro and lakeshore markets have been fast enough in recent years that strong photos plus pricing discipline have driven most sales. The listings that sat were not usually missing a video — they were missing competitive pricing or had photos that looked like they were shot on a phone. The floor is photography, not video. Video is the ceiling-raiser on the listings that have the ceiling to raise.

That is shifting slightly as more agents produce content and buyers get more media-literate, particularly in the $500K+ range where the West Michigan market has grown. But the fundamental math has not changed: a $245 photo package that makes a listing look sharp is still the highest-ROI line item in most listing budgets. Video is the right second investment after photos are handled.

How to decide for your specific listing

Three questions that make the call straightforward:

  • Is the buyer likely to be local, or do I need to sell the lifestyle to someone who cannot easily drive over? Remote or relocation buyers = video.
  • Is there something about this home that requires motion to communicate — a water view, a dramatic space, a floor plan that reads confusingly in stills? Motion-dependent features = video.
  • Am I actively building a social media presence and posting consistently? Personal brand play = agent reel regardless of listing price.

If you answer yes to at least one of those, video earns its place. If all three are no, invest in the best photo package you can and move fast on the list date.
