Who Owns Your Listing Photos? Usage Rights for Grand Rapids Agents

Kaden Elzinga November 4, 2026  ·  5 min read

An agent in Grand Rapids sells a house in March, then uses the same kitchen photo in an Instagram ad in June. Two weeks later, an email shows up from a photographer she never hired, asking for a licensing fee. The photo was on her listing, so she assumed it was hers. It wasn't.

Real estate photography usage rights trip up more agents than almost any other part of the job. Nobody talks about them until something goes wrong, so here is how it works.

The photographer owns the photos by default

Under U.S. copyright law, the person who presses the shutter owns the copyright from the moment the image exists. Paying for the shoot does not transfer that ownership. It buys you a license to use the photos, and the terms of that license are whatever you and the photographer agreed to.

The U.S. Copyright Office explains this in its general FAQ, and it applies to every photographer, from a big national company to a one-person studio. The takeaway is simple: unless a contract says otherwise, you are a licensee, not an owner.

That is not a bad thing. It just means the details live in the agreement, so the agreement is where you should look.

What a typical listing license covers

Most listing shoots come with a license to market that specific property. That usually means the MLS, your website, the brokerage site, and the major portals where the listing appears. It is the use everyone expects.

Where it gets fuzzy is everything past the listing. Social ads, postcards, a property brochure, your personal brand page, and a "just sold" video all live in the gray zone. If the terms don't mention them, don't assume they're allowed.

Where agents get into trouble

The most common problem is reusing old photos to promote yourself. A great living room shot from a listing that closed last year feels like a free asset, and it often is not.

The second is passing photos to someone else. Handing the gallery to a sister agent, a lender, or a stager so they can post it is a use you may not have the right to grant. The third is editing. Cropping out a watermark or running photos through an AI tool to change them can break the agreement and the photographer's trust.

What happens when the home sells

Here is the part sellers and agents rarely think about. After the home sells, the new owner may want those photos for their own records or for a future listing. They don't automatically have rights either.

The same goes for the old listing page. Photos can linger on portals long after closing, and if the license was tied to that one listing, a photographer can reasonably ask what is still being displayed. A clean agreement saves a lot of awkward emails.

Sellers also ask me about this when they want a copy of the gallery for a keepsake. A personal copy is a very different request from reusing the photos to market something, and it helps when the terms treat them differently.

Questions to ask before you book

You don't need a lawyer for this. You need four answers in writing, ideally before the shoot:

Who can use the photos besides me? Can the seller use them? Can I use them for my own marketing after the sale? And does the license have an end date?

If a photographer can't answer those in plain English, that is worth knowing before you hire them. The local MLS has its own rules about photos too, and you can see how that plays out in my guide to MLS photo requirements in West Michigan.

Why clear terms help everyone

Good terms protect you as much as the photographer. When the rules are written down, you can use your photos with confidence on your site, in your listing ads, and on social media without wondering who might email you later.

It also protects the seller. If the home takes longer to sell or comes back on the market, the photos are still usable without a scramble. I'd rather have a boring, clear agreement than a surprise in your inbox. That goes for your own brand marketing too, whether it is a headshot, a recap video, or a postcard to your sphere.
