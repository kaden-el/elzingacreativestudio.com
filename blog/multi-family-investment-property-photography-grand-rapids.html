Investment Property Photography for Duplexes and Multi-Family Listings in Grand Rapids

Kaden Elzinga August 26, 2026  ·  6 min read

A duplex on Fuller listed last spring with the same twelve photos you'd use for a single-family ranch: a front exterior, a living room, a kitchen, three bedrooms, done. It sat for 41 days. The agent finally added photos of both units side by side, the separate meters, and the shared driveway layout, and had an accepted offer inside two weeks. Nothing about the property changed. The photos just finally answered the questions an investor actually asks.

Grand Rapids has no shortage of these listings — Eastown, Baxter, Creston, and the blocks around GVSU's downtown campus are full of duplexes, triplexes, and small four-unit buildings that turn over constantly. Most of them get photographed like they're single-family homes, which undersells them to the buyer pool that actually wants them.

Investor Buyers Aren't Reading the Listing Like a Homebuyer

Someone shopping for a house to live in is scanning for light, layout, and whether the kitchen feels right. An investor is scanning for unit count, condition per unit, and whether the numbers pencil out before they've even opened the cap rate calculator.

That means the photo set has to do double duty. It still needs to be well-lit and well-composed, but it also needs to show the building as a system: how many doors, how the units are separated, what's shared and what isn't. A gorgeous photo of one unit's living room tells an investor nothing about the other three doors they're buying.

Shoot Every Unit, Not Just the Vacant One

The single biggest gap I see on multi-family listings is photographing only the empty unit because it's easier to access and stage. If a fourplex has three tenants and one vacancy, the listing photos end up representing 25 percent of the building.

Buyers making six-figure decisions want to see the condition of every unit they're buying, even the occupied ones. That takes more coordination with tenants and more shoot time, but it's the difference between a listing that reads as fully vetted and one that reads as a guess.

Show the Separation, Not Just the Space

Interior shots that matter for multi-family: separate entrances, separate meters or utility shutoffs, separate mailboxes, parking allocation, and any shared spaces like laundry or basement storage. These are the details that tell an investor how much work self-management will take versus how much a property manager will need to untangle.

A wide shot of the exterior showing both entrance doors clearly, plus a tight shot of the electrical panel or meter bank, does more for a serious buyer than another kitchen photo. It's not the prettiest frame in the gallery, but it's often the most-viewed one by the buyers who actually close.

Exterior Photos Should Read as a Building, Not a House

Single-family exteriors are shot to feel warm and inviting — golden light, a clean lawn, maybe a porch swing. Multi-family exteriors need to communicate scale and condition: roofline, siding condition across the whole structure, parking capacity, and how the lot is used.

A drone shot earns its keep here in a way it sometimes doesn't on a starter home. From above, a buyer can see lot size, parking count, and proximity to the street in one frame — details that would otherwise take three separate ground shots to establish. The <a href="https://www.faa.gov/uas">FAA's drone rules</a> still apply on these shoots the same as any residential listing, so any operator flying a duplex near downtown needs to be flying legally, not just carefully.

Floor Plans Matter More Here Than Anywhere Else

On a single-family listing, a floor plan is a nice-to-have. On a duplex or triplex, it's often the first thing a serious investor asks for, because unit layout and square footage per unit directly affect what rent each door can command.

A simple 2D floor plan per unit, even a basic one, answers questions that photos alone can't: is this a true 2-bed or a 1-bed with a converted den, does the layout allow for an in-unit washer/dryer, how does the flow work for a tenant. It's a relatively small add to a shoot that removes a round of buyer questions before they ever get asked.

Price the Shoot for the Building, Not the Bedroom Count

A four-unit building takes meaningfully longer to shoot than a four-bedroom house, even though a lot of agents book it the same way. Every unit needs its own interior pass, plus exterior and common-area coverage on top of that. Budgeting shoot time — and cost — around door count instead of bedroom count keeps expectations realistic for both the agent and the photographer.

It also means the add-ons that matter most shift. Drone coverage and a floor plan per unit tend to pay for themselves faster on multi-family than a twilight shot does, simply because they answer the questions this buyer pool is actually asking.

I've shot a fair number of duplexes and small multi-family buildings around Eastown and the west side over the past year, and the pattern holds every time: the listings that lean into showing the building as an investment, not a big house, are the ones that move. If you've got a multi-family listing coming up, it's worth planning the shot list around the buyer, not the square footage.
